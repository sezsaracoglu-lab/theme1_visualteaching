A note before you start

Sezen — this topic has a strange property that nothing else in your course has: you are the evidence. Every finding here is about a failure you cannot feel happening. You will read that half of people miss a gorilla walking through the middle of a video they are staring at, and your honest reaction will be "well, I would have seen it". You would probably not have. That gap between what your mind does and what you believe it does is not a footnote — it is the subject.

So the order matters. Lessons 1 to 9 show you what happens to the things attention leaves out. Lessons 10 to 18 open up the machine and ask how the gate works. Do not jump to the theories. They are four names that will slide straight off you unless you have first felt why anyone needed a theory at all.

How to read a lesson. Look at the picture first, for a good ten seconds, before you read a word. Then read the numbered points beside it — there are never more than five. Then read the italic line at the bottom, which is the thing to remember when everything else has faded. If a lesson takes you more than three minutes, you are reading too carefully; come back to it.

One promise: by lesson 18 you will be able to draw the whole topic on one page from memory. That is the actual test of whether you understand it.

Part one — what attention leaves out

LESSON 01the one idea everything rests on

Attention is a gate, and it is tiny

Your senses collect far more than your mind can use. Something has to choose. That something is attention.

  1. Your eyes and ears collect vastly more than your mind can handle. Nothing is filtered out by the sense organs — all of it arrives.
  2. Attention is the process that picks some of it for further processing and drops the rest. Think gatekeeper, not amplifier.
  3. The gate is small. Only a little gets through at any moment. This is called limited capacity.
  4. What gets through becomes an experience — you can report it, remember it, act on it. What does not gets no experience at all.
  5. So attention is not about seeing better. It is about seeing selectively, and the cost of that is the next eight lessons.
🔭

Remember this

Your senses are a wide funnel. Your mind is a narrow spout.

LESSON 02vocabulary you need before anything else

Four jobs we give the same gate

“Attention” is one word for four different tasks. Mixing them up is the single most common mistake in this topic.

  1. Selective attention — attend to one thing, block out the rest. This is the one the theories in Part two are about.
  2. Sustained attention, also called vigilance — the same skill, but held for a long time. Airport scanners, proofreading, a two-hour lecture.
  3. Divided attention — genuinely doing two things at once. Lesson 8 explains why this almost never happens.
  4. Task switching — hopping back and forth. This is not a synonym for divided attention; it is what actually happens when people think they are multitasking.
  5. All four draw on the same limited pool from lesson 1. That is why loading one job damages another.
🧭

Remember this

One spotlight. Point it, hold it, split it, or move it — those are your four options.

LESSON 03the most famous result in the topic

The invisible gorilla

Half the people watching a video missed a woman in a gorilla suit who stopped in the middle of the screen and thumped her chest.

  1. People watched a video of two teams passing a ball, and were told to count the passes by the white team. That is the job that eats their attention.
  2. Partway through, a woman in a gorilla suit walked in, faced the camera, thumped her chest and walked off — nine seconds, middle of the screen.
  3. About half never saw her. Across every condition of the study, 46% missed the unexpected event.
  4. This is inattentional blindness. Note that both conditions matter: the thing must be fully visible and unexpected.
  5. Nothing was wrong with their eyes. The gorilla landed perfectly on the retina. There was simply no attention left to turn it into an experience.
🦍

Remember this

The gorilla was never in the dark. Your torch was pointed somewhere else.

LESSON 04why the result feels impossible

Everyone is certain it would not happen to them

We are most confident about exactly the thing we are worst at. That gap is why this research matters practically.

  1. 90% of people say they would spot the gorilla. 78% of a national survey agreed that people generally notice unexpected things.
  2. The real numbers run the other way, and they get worse as the task gets harder or more realistic.
  3. Jogging past a staged fight at night, 65% missed it — a simulation of a real case where a police officer was convicted of lying for saying he had not seen a beating.
  4. Expertise does not save you. 83% of expert radiologists missed a gorilla forty-eight times the size of a lung nodule, inserted into a CT scan they were reading.
  5. The reason for the illusion is simple: you are vividly aware of what you noticed and completely unaware of what you missed. So your experience looks complete from the inside.
🪞

Remember this

You cannot notice what you failed to notice. That is why the confidence survives.

LESSON 05objective A2, answered

Three dials decide whether you notice

And “how obvious the thing is” is not one of them. That is the surprise.

  1. Similarity. If the surprise resembles what you are tracking, you notice it. Count the black team and the dark gorilla shows up; count the white team and it vanishes.
  2. Attentional load. The harder your real task, the less noticing. Counting bounce and aerial passes separately makes people miss more.
  3. Where attention is pointed. Without attention on the unexpected event, you are unlikely to become aware of it. No exceptions.
  4. Notice what is missing from the list: conspicuity. A bright red cross, the only colour on screen, visible for five seconds, was missed by 30% of people.
  5. So you are not filtering by "how much does this stand out". You are filtering by what you set yourself to look for — "white shirts" — and everything failing that description is dropped.
🎚️

Remember this

What you see is what you set. Not what shouts loudest.

LESSON 06the distinction that makes it all click

Looking is not seeing

Your eyes can be aimed straight at something while your attention is completely elsewhere. They are two separate things.

  1. There are two arrows in your head: where the eye points, and where attention points. Most beginners assume they are one arrow.
  2. They can come apart. Memmert (2006) recorded eye movements and found that people who missed the gorilla had fixated it for just as long as those who saw it.
  3. So the gorilla's image was on their retina, in focus, for seconds — and produced no experience.
  4. This is also why the limit is not visual at all: you can even miss a visual object while your resources are busy with a pure memory task.
  5. The practical version is Simons's own warning to cyclists: apparent eye contact is not evidence that the driver has seen you.
👁️

Remember this

The eye is the camera. Attention is the person watching the footage — and they can look away.

LESSON 07three failures students constantly confuse

Blindness, change blindness, and the blink

Three different ways awareness fails. The exam wants you to tell them apart, and the trick for doing so is embarrassingly simple.

  1. Inattentional blindness needs one image — the one containing the surprise. You fail to select it. Nothing to do with memory.
  2. Change blindness needs two images with a disruption between them. Because you must compare them, it also involves memory. That makes it a genuinely different mechanism.
  3. The famous change-blindness demo: someone asking for directions was swapped for a different person behind a passing door. Only 7 of 15 pedestrians noticed.
  4. The attentional blink is about time. Items flash past at ten a second; catch the first target and you are briefly blind to a second one arriving 200–500 ms later.
  5. Treating change blindness and inattentional blindness as the same thing is the commonest error in textbooks. Keep them separate.
📌
From outside your two readings. Your problem brief names change blindness and the attentional blink, but neither Simons nor Sternberg covers them. Say so if you use them — it shows you know where your sources end.
🔢

Remember this

One picture, two pictures, or a stopwatch. That is the whole distinction.

LESSON 08objective A4, answered

Multitasking is switching in disguise

You do not have two spotlights. You have one, moving fast, and every move costs you something.

  1. It feels like two beams on at once. It is one beam moving, and while it is on task B, task A is genuinely unattended.
  2. Each hop has a price — slower responses and more errors. That is why task switching is studied as its own topic in your lecture.
  3. The second task does not have to be in the same sense. A purely auditory distraction (a phone call) produces a purely visual failure. One pool, many senses.
  4. Simons is blunt about it: "Although people believe they can multitask, few can."
  5. The dangerous part is the next lesson: you cannot feel the cost while you are paying it.
🔀

Remember this

One torch, two rooms. You sweep between them and call it "being in both".

LESSON 09why any of this matters outside a lab

The unicycling clown

Someone put a clown on a unicycle in the middle of a university campus and counted who noticed. The answer depends entirely on what was in people's hands.

  1. A clown on a unicycle rode across a path. Only 25% of people on the phone reported seeing it, against 51% walking alone and 61% with music.
  2. The surprise is the last bar: people walking in a pair noticed most (71%). Talking is not the problem.
  3. What makes a phone worse is that the partner is absent — holding up a conversation with someone who cannot see your situation demands more of the central pool.
  4. The same logic applies to driving: phone conversation "decreases situation awareness and increases the chances that people will miss something important".
  5. And the killer detail: drivers do not notice how distracted they are. Your judgement of your own capacity fails at the same moment your capacity does.
💡
Use this if asked "why are phones worse than passengers?" — the pairs result answers it directly, and most students only remember the 25% figure.
🤡

Remember this

A conversation with someone who can see the road is cheap. One with someone who cannot is expensive.

Part two — how the gate actually works

LESSON 10the experiment behind every theory that follows

The cocktail party, in a laboratory

Everything in Part two comes out of one simple task. Learn the task now and the four theories become easy.

  1. The real-life puzzle is the cocktail party problem — following one conversation while others compete (Cherry, 1953).
  2. He tested it with dichotic presentation: a different message played to each ear. "Dichotic" just means split in two.
  3. And shadowing: repeat one message out loud as it arrives, like a detective shadowing a suspect. This is the task, not the finding.
  4. People manage it, with effort. So selective attention works.
  5. The whole of Part two is then about one question: what happened to the ignored ear? Every theory is an answer to that.
🎧

Remember this

Two ears, two messages, repeat one. That single task generated four theories.

LESSON 11the single most important pattern in Part two

Sound survives. Meaning does not.

Probe the ignored ear afterwards and you find a very specific pattern. Every theory that follows exists to explain this one asymmetry.

  1. Change something physical in the ignored ear — a male voice becoming female, speech becoming a tone — and people notice.
  2. Change the meaning — English to German, speech reversed, content turned to nonsense — and they do not.
  3. So the ignored ear is not switched off. Its sound is still being registered; only its sense is not.
  4. Simons's phrase for it is cognitive deafness: your ear is working perfectly, your mind has simply stopped decoding that channel.
  5. Every theory in the next four lessons is an attempt to say where in the pipeline this happens, and how.
🔇

Remember this

You keep the noise and lose the news.

LESSON 12the exception that broke the first theory

Except your own name

One word gets through the gate when nothing else does. And the people it gets through for are not the ones you would guess.

  1. Ordinary words in the ignored ear go nowhere. Your own name gets through for about one person in three (Moray, 1959).
  2. Why? It is enormously overlearned and personally loaded. It is the one piece of content strong enough to force the gate.
  3. Now the twist. The people who catch it have lower memory span: 65% of low-span people heard it, against only 20% of high-span people.
  4. The authors' explanation: high-span people are simply better at blocking the irrelevant message. Their filter holds.
  5. So noticing your name is a failure of selective attention that happens to be useful — not a sign that your attention is sharp.
⚠️
Be careful here. A preregistered replication (Röer & Cowan, 2021) reproduced the name effect but found no reliable link to memory span. Say the effect is solid and the individual-difference part is contested.
📣

Remember this

Your name is the one word loud enough to break a window. Some windows are just thinner.

LESSON 13theory 1 of 4

Broadbent: a shutter, early

The first attempt. One channel gets in, chosen by how it sounds, and everything else stops at the door.

  1. Broadbent's picture is a pipeline: senses → filter → perception → short-term memory → response. Learn the pipeline; all four theories reuse it.
  2. The filter sits early, right after the senses and before anything is understood.
  3. It chooses on physical features alone — loudness, pitch, accent. It lets exactly one channel through; the rest "may never reach the level of perception".
  4. This explains lesson 11 beautifully: a tone is a physical change and gets noticed; English→German needs meaning, and meaning is behind the filter.
  5. It is killed by one result: your own name (lesson 12). A shut door cannot let a name through.
🚪

Remember this

A bouncer who checks how you look, lets one group in, and never learns anyone's name.

LESSON 14theory 2 of 4 — and why it was not enough

Moray: the same shutter, but leaky

The minimum possible repair. Keep Broadbent's door exactly where it is, and punch a small hole in it.

  1. Moray keeps Broadbent's filter in the same place and just makes it permeable: "some personally important messages are so powerful that they burst through".
  2. That handles single special words like your name. It handles nothing else.
  3. Treisman's switch study: she moved the rest of a sentence into the ignored ear, and people carried on into the wrong ear for a word or two. You can only do that if you were tracking the meaning.
  4. The twin study: everyone spotted it when both ears carried the same message — a filter passing only one channel could never detect a match.
  5. The translation study: bilinguals noticed when the ignored ear was a translation. Two messages with nothing acoustic in common can only be matched at the level of meaning. That is fatal.
🩹

Remember this

A hole in the door lets one name through. It cannot let a whole translation through.

LESSON 15theory 3 of 4 — the one that lasted

Treisman: a volume knob, not a shutter

Her move is the most important idea in Part two. Stop asking whether the other channel is processed and start asking how much.

  1. Treisman replaces the shutter with a volume knob: "instead of blocking stimuli out, the filter merely weakens (attenuates)" everything that is not the target.
  2. The order matters. First a cheap check for target properties (loudness, pitch). Signals that pass go on at full strength; everything else goes on weakened.
  3. Then meaning is analysed — for both channels. So the ignored channel does get understood, faintly.
  4. That single change explains all three of her results at once: a faint signal can still surface if it is important enough.
  5. The conceptual leap: she stops asking whether the unattended channel is processed and asks how much. Degree, not all-or-nothing.
🔊

Remember this

Everyone gets into the club. The people not on the list just arrive muffled.

LESSON 16theory 4 of 4

Deutsch & Deutsch: move the door to the end

A more radical answer. Do not weaken anything — understand everything, and only then decide what reaches you.

  1. Same pipeline, but the filter has moved to the end. Stimuli are filtered out "only after they have been analysed for both their physical properties and their meaning".
  2. Look at the input arrows: both are solid. Nothing is blocked and nothing is weakened on the way in.
  3. This gets the name effect, the twin message and the translation for free — by the time selection happens, everything has already been understood.
  4. The price is efficiency. You do the expensive work of understanding material you are about to discard.
  5. Crucially, it is the same family as Broadbent: one bottleneck, one stream. The argument is only about where the bottleneck sits.
🏢

Remember this

The door moved to the far end of the corridor. Everyone walks in; one person gets to speak to you.

LESSON 17objectives B4 and B5, in one picture

All four, side by side

Two questions separate every theory in this topic. Answer them and you have named any of the four.

  1. Question 1 — where? Broadbent, Moray and Treisman all put the gate early, before meaning. Deutsch & Deutsch and Norman put it late, after.
  2. Question 2 — what does it do? Broadbent and Deutsch & Deutsch block. Moray blocks but leaks. Treisman weakens.
  3. Read the empty cell: a late filter has no need to leak or attenuate, because meaning is already extracted. That is why nobody proposed one.
  4. Each theory exists because of a result that killed the previous one. Learn it as a chain: sounds-only → but my name → but translations → so let us move the door.
  5. The hardest pair to separate is Treisman vs Deutsch & Deutsch, because both let unattended meaning matter. The difference is how much analysis the ignored channel gets: degraded and partial, versus full and complete.
🎯
If you only memorise one thing for the exam: "one bottleneck, and they argue about where it sits and what it does to what fails." Every comparison question is answerable from that sentence.
🚪

Remember this

One nightclub, one door. Where does it stand, and how hard is it to get past?

LESSON 18how the argument was actually settled

Both sides were right, at different times

The early-versus-late debate was not won. It was dissolved — by noticing that the gate moves.

  1. Neisser (1967) stopped looking for one filter and proposed two processes: fast parallel preattentive ones that see only physical features, and slow serial attentive ones that build meaning.
  2. That split is the ancestor of the automatic-versus-controlled distinction you meet all through cognitive psychology.
  3. Lavie (1995) then finished the job: the gate is not in a fixed place. Its position moves with how hard your task is.
  4. High load uses up capacity, so distractors are never perceived — you get Broadbent's result. Low load leaves spare capacity, distractors get in, and selection must happen late — you get Deutsch & Deutsch's result.
  5. Which means both sides were right, about different conditions. And it loops straight back to dial 2 in lesson 5 — the same idea, arriving from the other direction.
📖
From outside your two readings. Sternberg stops at Neisser (1967). Lavie is the modern resolution — present it as the dominant framework, not a settled fact, since the mechanism is still debated.
⚖️

Remember this

Nobody won. The gate slides — and how hard you are working decides where it stops.

Every term, in one line each

Attention
The process that selects some of what your senses collect for further processing, and drops the rest.
Limited capacity
Only a small amount can be processed at any one moment. The reason everything else in this topic happens.
Selective attention
Attending to one thing on purpose while blocking the rest. The cocktail-party skill.
Sustained attention (vigilance)
The same skill held over a long stretch of time.
Divided attention
Genuinely doing two things at once. Mostly does not happen; it is switching instead.
Task switching
Hopping between tasks. Not a synonym for divided attention, and it costs time and accuracy each hop.
Inattentional blindness
Failing to notice something fully visible but unexpected, because attention was elsewhere.
Inattentional deafness
The same thing in hearing — missing an unexpected voice while attention is on another sound.
Change blindness
Missing a large change to a scene when it happens during a blink or blank. Needs two views, so it involves memory.
Attentional blink
Catching one target makes you briefly blind to a second arriving 200–500 ms later. A failure in time.
Attentional load
How much of your capacity the task you were actually given is consuming.
Cocktail party problem
Following one conversation while others compete for your ears (Cherry, 1953).
Dichotic presentation
Playing a different message to each ear. A method, not a finding.
Shadowing
Repeating the attended message out loud, word for word, as it arrives.
Filter
Something that blocks part of the information so only some passes to the next stage.
Bottleneck
Something that slows information passing through. All four theories propose one.
Attenuation
Weakening rather than blocking. Treisman's key move: the ignored channel is turned down, not off.
Early vs late selection
Whether the gate acts before meaning is extracted or after. The single biggest dividing line.
Preattentive processes
Automatic, rapid, parallel. Notice physical features only — no meaning (Neisser, 1967).
Perceptual load theory
The gate's position moves with task difficulty. High load → early selection; low load → late (Lavie, 1995).
